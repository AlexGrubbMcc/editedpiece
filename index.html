Earliest uses: Histoire de Mr. Vieux Bois

Rodolphe Töpffer's Histoire de Mr. Vieux Bois is an early milestone in modern comics. He drew the story in 1827, and it was first published in Geneva in 1837. It follows a man's comic attempts at romance through a sequence of pictures and short captions.

The same character appears from one scene to the next. Frames separate the actions, while the captions help connect them. Instead of one picture explaining a whole event, several pictures carry the story forward. This relationship between words, images, and panels is still central to comics today.

The dream illustration beside this text comes from this early work. Its American adaptation, The Adventures of Mr. Obadiah Oldbuck, is described by Dartmouth as the first comic book printed in the United States. Töpffer's work is an important early example, although the title of the very first modern comic depends on how the format is defined.

Manga refers to Japanese comics, while manhwa refers to Korean comics. Both have histories that reach beyond the few works on this page. Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy began serialization in April 1952 and is one useful postwar manga example. A Korean government history points to Lee Do-yeong's newspaper cartoon in 1909 as an early milestone in modern Korean comics.

During the 2000s, Korean webtoons developed around digital reading. A long scrolling screen offered a different canvas from a printed page. Today, many webtoons use vertical space to delay a reveal or separate moments. This does not mean all manhwa scrolls, or that every manga follows the same layout.

Comics, manga, and manhwa

These are examples of storytelling traditions, not three separate genres or fixed rules.

Comics / Spider-Man

Amazing Fantasy #15

Spider-Man first appeared in this 1962 comic, written by Stan Lee and drawn by Steve Ditko. Peter Parker gains powers from a radioactive spider and learns that being a hero brings responsibility. The printed story uses panels to move between action, dialogue, and reactions.

Manga

One Piece

Eiichiro Oda's manga follows Monkey D. Luffy and his pirate crew as they search for the One Piece treasure. Its Japanese reading order runs from right to left. Small panels can carry conversations and reactions, while larger scenes give adventures more room.

Manhwa / Webtoon

Omniscient Reader's Viewpoint

Known as Omniscient Reader on WEBTOON, this adaptation credits singNsong, UMI, and Sleepy-C. Dokja's favorite web novel becomes reality. The vertical format lets readers move down through the story, with space between images helping separate each moment.

Why call it a bento box?

A bento meal box holds different things in separate compartments. This is a useful comparison for panels arranged together, not a historical name for all comic layouts.

Some moments need more room.

A large panel can make an arrival, a setting, or an important reaction stand out.

A glance

A small panel can isolate one expression or detail.

A pause

The gap between panels is called a gutter. It separates the moments.

Keep moving

Panel order leads the reader through the story, even when the sizes change.

Stretching the form

From a page to a scroll

A splash page can give one image almost the whole page. A manga spread can carry a scene across two pages. A vertical webtoon can stretch the distance before the next image. In each case, the artist changes the space available to a moment.

The size should mean something

Unequal panels are useful when the change supports emphasis or pacing. This page borrows that idea: the history has more room than a short example, and the main demonstration panel is larger than its supporting details.

Dos and don'ts

Do: guide the reader

Keep the reading order clear, leave space around the lettering, and use a larger panel when the story earns it. Small panels work well for short actions and reactions. Let the layout support the story.

Don't: fill every gap

Crowded speech balloons and too many competing panels make a page tiring. A tiny panel can hide an important detail, especially on a phone. Avoid making every moment equally loud or assuming that one reading direction suits every audience.
